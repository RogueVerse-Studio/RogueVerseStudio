The God of Manga: How Osamu Tezuka Changed Manga Forever

He did not invent manga. He did something more interesting: he helped expand what postwar story manga could look like, what it could talk about, and how far its characters could travel beyond the printed page.

RogueVerse Manga History

The God of Manga

Osamu Tezuka helped turn manga into cinematic long-form storytelling, pushed television anime into a new era, built an animation empire, watched parts of it collapse, reinvented himself, and kept asking one question across decades of work: what is a life worth?

Pick up a modern manga.

It almost does not matter which one.

A battle manga can cut from a city-wide establishing shot to a character's terrified eye. A romance can devote several panels to silence before anyone speaks. A horror manga can make you turn a page before revealing what is waiting on the other side. A science-fiction series can spend hundreds of chapters asking whether artificial life deserves the same dignity as humanity.

None of those techniques feels revolutionary anymore.

That may be one of the greatest compliments history can give Osamu Tezuka. His influence became ordinary.

Long before manga became a global industry capable of producing Dragon Ball, One Piece, Berserk, Monster, Naruto, Death Note, Demon Slayer, Chainsaw Man and thousands of other works, Tezuka spent decades exploring just how much storytelling could fit between two panels.

Japan eventually gave him an almost absurdly large nickname: Manga no Kamisama, the God of Manga.

Important distinction: Tezuka did not invent manga. Japanese comics and narrative cartoon traditions existed before he was born, and many artists contributed to the medium's development. His importance lies in how powerfully he expanded, popularized and industrialized forms of postwar story manga and animation.

Before the God

Osamu Tezuka was born on November 3, 1928, in Toyonaka, Osaka Prefecture, and grew up in Takarazuka. Long before professional deadlines swallowed his life, several obsessions were already forming: insects, movies, theater, comics and eventually medicine.

His fascination with insects became so intense that it even influenced the name by which the world would know him. His childhood environment also exposed him to the theatrical spectacle of the Takarazuka Revue, whose extravagant costumes, heightened romance and gender performance would later echo through his work.

Cinema mattered just as much. Tezuka grew up consuming animation and film, and that fascination seeped into the way he drew comics. Characters did not merely occupy panels. Scenes could be staged. A face could become a close-up. A location could be introduced through something resembling an establishing shot. Movement could be broken across consecutive images.

A manga page could behave almost like a movie trapped on paper.

War and the Question That Would Not Leave Him

Then history became considerably uglier.

Tezuka experienced wartime Japan as a teenager. He lived through the destruction and deprivation surrounding World War II and the bombing of Osaka. His official biography later connected those experiences with one of the defining ideas running throughout his career: the preciousness of life.

That sounds gentle until you read enough Tezuka.

His stories repeatedly test the value of life against war, prejudice, nationalism, technology, environmental destruction, disease and humanity's talent for killing itself. He was not simply writing “be nice to each other.” He spent decades asking why humans find that apparently simple instruction so difficult.

The Doctor Who Chose Manga

Tezuka also studied medicine. This was not honorary-doctor trivia added to his biography later. He genuinely pursued medical training and qualified as a physician.

Yet manga kept pulling him back.

That collision between medicine and art eventually became enormously important. Decades later, Black Jack would turn surgery, mortality and medical ethics into some of Tezuka's most famous storytelling.

Before Black Jack could pick up a scalpel, however, Tezuka had to become a manga artist.

1947: The Treasure Island Explosion

Tezuka's early professional work appeared shortly after World War II. Then came New Treasure Island in 1947, produced with Shichima Sakai.

This is one of the points where Tezuka mythology can become dangerously neat. A simplified version of manga history sometimes claims that Tezuka essentially invented cinematic manga with this book.

Reality is messier. Japanese cartoonists were experimenting with narrative and visual techniques before Tezuka. New Treasure Island itself was collaborative, making Sakai's contribution essential to the story rather than decorative historical fine print.

What the work unquestionably became, however, was a major early landmark in Tezuka's rise. Its storytelling felt energetic and expansive to young postwar readers.

Rather than treating every panel as a box containing the next piece of information, Tezuka increasingly treated sequential images as units of time. A vehicle could move through multiple panels. A character's reaction could deserve its own image. Distance mattered. Angles mattered. Pacing mattered.

Readers were not merely told what happened. They were pulled through it.

The Science-Fiction Laboratory

Tezuka followed his early success with ambitious science-fiction stories including Lost World, Metropolis and Nextworld.

Look at the subjects he was already circling: technology, civilization, war, evolution, artificial humanity, scientific ambition and the possibility that mankind might become powerful enough to destroy itself.

The cheerful cartoonist was already building doomsday machines in the basement.

Then one small robot changed everything.

Enter Atom

Before he became the star of his own manga, Atom appeared in Ambassador Atom. Tezuka recognized the character's potential, and in 1952 Mighty Atom began serialization.

Western audiences would eventually know him by another name: Astro Boy.

The premise is deceptively simple. A brilliant scientist loses his son and creates an advanced robot boy in his image. But a machine cannot truly replace a dead child. Atom eventually finds a different place in the world, becoming a heroic figure caught between humanity and artificial life.

Underneath the rockets, super-strength and adventure stories sat an idea that Tezuka would revisit endlessly: what makes a life worthy of respect?

Humans in Astro Boy can be compassionate. They can also be spectacularly cruel. Robots can behave heroically. Humans can treat them as property. Technology itself is not automatically salvation or damnation. The moral problem usually returns to the person deciding how that technology will be used.

That question remains remarkably alive in an era of artificial intelligence, autonomous weapons and increasingly humanlike machines.

When Manga Entered Television

Tezuka founded Mushi Production and pushed aggressively into animation. On January 1, 1963, the television version of Astro Boy premiered.

Calling it “the first anime ever” is wrong. Japanese animation existed decades earlier. Even television animation preceded it in other formats.

What Astro Boy became was something more specific and enormously consequential. Tezuka's official archive describes it as Japan's first 30-minute television animation series. The original run ultimately produced 193 episodes, and the series became a nationwide phenomenon before reaching audiences overseas.

Producing approximately half an hour of television animation every week was a completely different beast from making occasional theatrical animation. The schedule was merciless. The economics were difficult.

The solution was limited animation. Frames could be reused. Movement could be suggested rather than fully animated. A still drawing could become dramatic through camera movement, editing, sound or composition. Mouths could move while the rest of a character remained stationary. Cycles could repeat.

Anime would eventually turn limitations like these into an entire visual vocabulary.

The Cost of Building Television Anime

There was a cost.

Tezuka's approach to television animation economics remains controversial. He has often been blamed for establishing budgets that undervalued animation production and contributed to an industry culture in which animators have historically been underpaid.

That argument should not be reduced to “Tezuka caused low anime wages.” Modern anime labor economics developed through decades of changing broadcasters, studios, contractors, production committees and business practices.

But neither should the uncomfortable part be erased. Tezuka wanted weekly animation badly enough to accept a business model that was extraordinarily difficult to sustain.

And eventually, it did not.

Before the collapse came the explosion. Astro Boy traveled internationally. Jungle Emperor, known to many Western viewers through Kimba the White Lion, reached television. Princess Knight received animation. Characters moved from manga pages into television, merchandise and international pop culture.

Tezuka was helping demonstrate the power of characters as multimedia properties.

The Princess Who Would Not Stay in Her Box

If Astro Boy demonstrates Tezuka's influence on science fiction and animation, Princess Knight reveals another side of his career.

The original version began serialization in Shojo Club in 1953. Its heroine, Sapphire, is born into a kingdom where women cannot inherit the throne. She therefore must live publicly as a prince.

Tezuka's official archive explicitly connects Sapphire's conception and visual presentation with the Takarazuka Revue that surrounded his childhood.

The work became enormously influential in discussions of early narrative shōjo manga. But once again, the mythology needs a footnote. Tezuka did not single-handedly invent shōjo manga. Other artists played crucial roles in creating what eventually became shōjo manga's distinctive visual and emotional language.

The more defensible claim is that Princess Knight became one of its foundational landmarks.

The Cute Drawings Were a Trap

Someone discovering Tezuka only through pictures of Astro Boy might reasonably assume his bibliography is dominated by cheerful children's adventure.

Then you start reading deeper.

Dororo follows Hyakkimaru, whose body has been sacrificed to supernatural beings by his father and who must fight to reclaim what was taken from him.

Ayako crawls through a poisonous postwar family and the corruption surrounding it. MW enters psychological, political and sexual territory far removed from children's television. Ode to Kirihito turns disease and medicine into a disturbing examination of dehumanization. Message to Adolf uses intertwined lives to examine nationalism, fascism, race and World War II. Buddha transforms the life of Siddhartha Gautama into a sprawling human drama.

And then there is Black Jack.

Tezuka's ability to move between children's adventure, science fiction, historical drama, medical fiction, horror, romance, crime, philosophy and adult psychological storytelling is one of the strongest arguments for his importance.

Manga could be about almost anything.

The Secret Tezuka Cinematic Universe That Wasn't One

Modern audiences are trained to recognize shared universes. A character walks into another hero's movie and everybody begins searching for the crossover explanation.

Tezuka did something stranger. He had a Star System.

Recurring Tezuka character designs could appear in completely different stories playing completely different people. A familiar hero might be recast as a supporting character. A friendly face could return as a villain. Continuity was not necessarily the point.

Casting was.

Tezuka imagined his fictional characters as performers. He was the director. They were his actors. His official archive explains that he even invented imaginary appearance fees for members of his fictional repertory company.

It is wonderfully eccentric. It is also revealing. Tezuka was not merely borrowing the visual language of cinema. He was thinking like a filmmaker while making comics.

Then the God Started Losing

This may be the most important part of the story, because gods are not supposed to become obsolete.

Tezuka almost did.

By the 1960s and early 1970s, manga was changing rapidly. The gekiga movement had helped establish darker, more realistic and psychologically intense comics. New generations of artists were creating work that looked and felt different from the round, theatrical visual language associated with Tezuka.

Readers were growing older. Their tastes were changing. The manga ecosystem Tezuka helped expand was now producing creators who could challenge him.

Meanwhile, his animation ambitions were becoming financially brutal. Mushi Production encountered serious difficulties and ultimately went bankrupt in 1973.

Imagine the symbolism. The man credited with helping establish weekly television anime watched the studio behind that revolution collapse.

Tezuka's status did not magically protect him from economics. Creatively, he also knew tastes were shifting. There were periods when his work was viewed as old-fashioned.

The God of Manga could bleed.

Then a mysterious surgeon walked into the room.

Black Jack: The Comeback

Black Jack began in 1973. Its central character is an unlicensed surgeon of almost supernatural ability. He can perform seemingly impossible procedures. He charges outrageous prices. He can appear cold, arrogant and mercenary.

And then Tezuka complicates him. Again. And again. And again.

The manga uses medicine as a pressure chamber for morality.

What is a human life worth? Does saving someone make that person good? Should every life be saved? What happens when medicine becomes business? When does a physician stop being healer and start playing God? Who gets to decide?

Tezuka's medical education suddenly became narrative ammunition.

The creator who had once sent a rocket-powered robot child into battle was now using scalpels, disease and impossible decisions to interrogate the same fundamental idea: what does it mean to value life?

Black Jack became one of his signature works and helped demonstrate that Tezuka could still capture readers after the manga landscape had changed around him.

Phoenix: The Work He Could Never Finish

If Astro Boy is Tezuka's most recognizable creation, Phoenix may be the closest thing to his artistic thesis. Tezuka's official archive calls it his life's work.

Across stories separated by vast stretches of history and future time, Phoenix repeatedly circles immortality, reincarnation, civilization, suffering and the value of existence.

Human beings pursue eternal life. They destroy themselves trying to possess it. Civilizations rise. Civilizations fall. Technology advances. Human desire remains stubbornly familiar.

The structure itself became ambitious. Rather than simply marching chronologically toward the future, different stories move across eras, creating the feeling that all human history is being squeezed between the distant past and distant future.

Tezuka never completed the ultimate design.

That incompleteness feels almost appropriate. A manga about humanity's futile obsession with escaping death was left unfinished because its creator could not escape death either.

Tezuka died on February 9, 1989. He was 60 years old.

He left behind unfinished work, but not an unfinished influence.

The Part of the Legacy That Should Make Us Uncomfortable

Historical importance does not require sainthood.

Some Tezuka works contain racial caricatures, stereotypes and representations that are uncomfortable or offensive to modern readers. Certain gender and cultural portrayals belong unmistakably to the eras in which they were created.

His animation business decisions remain part of ongoing debates about the economics of Japanese television animation.

Some claims surrounding his career have also grown larger with repetition.

Did he invent manga? No.

Did he invent anime? Absolutely not.

Did he invent shōjo manga? That claim is too simplistic.

Was every cinematic technique in manga his invention? No.

Was every aspect of the anime industry descended directly from Astro Boy? No.

Stripping those myths away does not make Tezuka smaller. It makes the real achievement easier to see.

The Tezuka DNA

Manga can control time

A sequence does not need to race from plot point to plot point. Panels can create pauses. Movement can unfold across images. A reaction can matter as much as the action causing it.

Cartoon characters can carry adult ideas

A drawing does not need photorealism to discuss death, racism, war, sexuality, religion or medical ethics. That principle is practically foundational to manga and anime today.

Genre boundaries are invitations

Tezuka could move from children's science fiction to medical drama to Buddhist biography to psychological thriller. Modern manga's staggering genre diversity feels completely natural now. It was not always inevitable.

Characters can outgrow their original medium

Astro Boy became manga, television animation, merchandise, international entertainment and a cultural icon. Today that path is practically an industry strategy.

Manga creators can think cinematically

Modern manga artists routinely discuss camera angles, staging, cuts and visual rhythm. Readers barely notice because the grammar is now familiar.

Popular entertainment can ask enormous questions

Tezuka's stories repeatedly return to war, technology, prejudice, ecological destruction, mortality and the dignity of life. Entertainment and philosophy were never mutually exclusive.

PLUTO: The Perfect Proof

Perhaps the best demonstration of Tezuka's survival is not an Astro Boy remake. It is PLUTO.

Naoki Urasawa took “The Greatest Robot on Earth,” a famous Astro Boy storyline, and rebuilt it into an adult science-fiction murder mystery.

The transformation is radical. Atom exists, but the visual world is unmistakably Urasawa's. Characters who once belonged to Tezuka's cartoon universe are reconstructed with psychological and political weight suited to a completely different era.

The manga ran from 2003 to 2009, and its eventual 2023 anime adaptation introduced another generation of viewers to Tezuka's ideas, sometimes without those viewers initially realizing they were watching descendants of Astro Boy.

That is legacy in its healthiest form.

Not preservation under glass.

Mutation.

A later master looked at an older master's work and asked: what else can this become?

That question might be the most Tezuka-like response possible.

So Why Call Him the God of Manga?

Not because he created manga. He did not.

Not because every convention started with him. It did not.

Not because every work aged perfectly. It has not.

And not because manga would somehow have ceased to exist without him. It would not.

The title makes sense for a different reason.

Tezuka spent more than four decades repeatedly testing the boundaries of popular visual storytelling. He helped make long-form story manga a dominant cultural force. He moved freely across genres and demographics. He helped demonstrate the potential of manga characters as animation and merchandising properties. He pushed television animation at a scale that changed Japanese broadcasting.

He built a studio.

He watched that studio fail.

He faced a generation that threatened to make him obsolete.

He came back.

He produced medical drama, children's adventure, historical fiction, science fiction, horror, romance and philosophical epics.

And until the end, he kept drawing.

The most revealing part of Tezuka's influence may therefore be what happens when a modern reader opens a manga and does not think about him at all.

A cinematic close-up does not scream “Tezuka.” A silent reaction panel does not scream “Tezuka.” A cute character confronting horrific subject matter does not scream “Tezuka.” A manga becoming an anime, game, toy line and international franchise does not scream “Tezuka.”

Those things simply feel like manga.

That is what happens when influence sinks deep enough. The fingerprints disappear into the object.

Osamu Tezuka did not create manga. He helped teach modern manga what it was capable of becoming.
