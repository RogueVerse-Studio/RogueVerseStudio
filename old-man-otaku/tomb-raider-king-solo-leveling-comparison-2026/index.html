Tomb Raider King Is More Than a Solo Leveling Replacement, and That Comparison May Be Hurting It

Crunchyroll’s newest Korean action-fantasy has all the ingredients needed to trigger Solo Leveling comparisons. But beneath the supernatural artifacts and overwhelmingly capable protagonist is a very different power fantasy built around regression, revenge, information and getting to the treasure first.

By RogueVerse Studio | August 5, 2026

Spoiler level: Light spoilers for the opening premise and early episodes.

There may be no faster way to get an action-fantasy series noticed in 2026 than calling it “the next Solo Leveling.”

There may also be no faster way to make audiences judge it for everything it is not.

That is the uncomfortable position currently occupied by Tomb Raider King, the new South Korean animated adaptation streaming on Crunchyroll.

The comparisons were practically inevitable.

Its protagonist is a dark-haired Korean man operating in a modern world transformed by supernatural phenomena. Mysterious locations appear across the planet. Those locations contain objects capable of granting extraordinary powers. People enter them in pursuit of wealth and influence. A disastrous expedition leaves the protagonist facing death, only for something impossible to happen that gives him an enormous advantage over everyone around him.

Put those ingredients next to Solo Leveling and the family resemblance is difficult to ignore.

Some viewers have gone considerably further than noticing similarities.

Early online discussion has included accusations that Tomb Raider King is essentially a Solo Leveling clone with tombs replacing dungeons and relics replacing artifacts. That reaction is understandable. It is also incomplete.

Because the deeper Tomb Raider King moves into its actual premise, the less useful the Solo Leveling comparison becomes.

The two stories may shop at the same supernatural hardware store, but they are building different houses.

What Is Tomb Raider King?

Tomb Raider King began as a South Korean web novel by SAN.G before receiving a manhwa adaptation by Yuns of REDICE STUDIO with artwork by 3B2S. Yen Press publishes the English edition of the manhwa.

Its world changes when mysterious tombs begin appearing across the globe. These are not ordinary archaeological discoveries. Inside are ancient supernatural relics capable of granting their owners extraordinary abilities.

Humanity responds about as gracefully as humanity normally does whenever an object capable of rearranging the balance of power appears. People fight over them. Corporations attempt to monopolize them. The wealthy become even more powerful. Governments and organizations become interested. Raiders emerge to locate, enter and exploit tombs.

A completely new economy forms around supernatural artifacts.

At the center of that system is Jooheon Suh.

Jooheon is an experienced raider who has spent years working inside the dangerous world created by the tombs. Then his employer betrays him and leaves him to die.

Instead, Jooheon returns approximately fifteen years into the past.

The tombs have not yet transformed civilization. The major players have not accumulated their supernatural arsenals. People who will eventually control priceless relics do not even know those objects exist yet.

But Jooheon remembers.

That is the real hook of Tomb Raider King. This is not simply a story about becoming powerful. It is a story about knowing where power will appear before everybody else.

Solo Leveling Is About Ascension. Tomb Raider King Is About Preemption.

The easiest way to explain the difference between Solo Leveling and Tomb Raider King is to look at the relationship each protagonist has with knowledge.

Sung Jinwoo begins near the bottom. His reputation is terrible. His physical ability is limited. Danger surrounds him. When the System changes his life, neither Jinwoo nor the audience fully understands where it will ultimately take him.

That uncertainty powers much of early Solo Leveling.

Jooheon is different.

His second journey begins with information. He already lived through the future. He remembers people, betrayals, relics and the emerging power structure.

That turns Tomb Raider King into something closer to supernatural New Game Plus.

Imagine replaying a massive role-playing game while retaining your memory of every secret weapon, hidden boss, rare item and future betrayal. The townspeople are still explaining how shops work. You already know where the legendary equipment is buried.

That is Jooheon’s advantage.

His progression therefore does not deliver exactly the same fantasy as watching Jinwoo become stronger. Jooheon is trying to arrive first.

And sometimes, arriving first is more powerful than having the biggest sword.

Knowledge Becomes a Superpower

One of the most interesting ideas inside Tomb Raider King is that information has tangible value.

Suppose two people are equally capable of retrieving a powerful relic. One knows the relic will exist five years before the other person does. They are not actually equal.

The person with information controls the board.

Jooheon’s future memories allow him to identify valuable opportunities before society understands their importance. That produces something resembling supernatural insider trading.

A tomb that appears unimportant to everyone else may contain something Jooheon remembers becoming priceless. A person who appears harmless today may become dangerous later. A company that seems insignificant may eventually control enormous influence.

An enemy who believes they are meeting Jooheon for the first time may already be dealing with someone who remembers exactly what they did to him in another timeline.

That is deliciously unfair.

And unfairness is part of the appeal.

Power fantasies do not always work because audiences want perfectly balanced conflicts. Sometimes they work because audiences enjoy watching somebody walk into a trap carrying the instruction manual.

Jooheon Is Also a Different Kind of Protagonist

This may be the biggest reason some viewers bounce off Tomb Raider King.

Jooheon is not especially interested in behaving like a traditional shining hero. He can be ruthless. He can be opportunistic. He carries grudges.

His knowledge of the future does not automatically turn him into a benevolent guardian of humanity. Sometimes he helps people. Sometimes he manipulates situations. Sometimes his motivations are deeply personal.

That personality produces a very different relationship between protagonist and viewer.

Sung Jinwoo’s coolness increasingly emerges from capability. Jooheon’s appeal often emerges from audacity.

You are not necessarily watching because you want him to save everyone. You may be watching because you want to see what outrageous advantage he exploits next.

For some viewers, that makes him entertaining. For others, it makes him difficult to connect with.

But Yes, the Solo Leveling Similarities Are Real

Defending Tomb Raider King as its own story does not require pretending the comparisons are imaginary.

Both properties come from the broader Korean web-fiction and manhwa ecosystem. Both operate in modern settings disrupted by supernatural systems. Both involve dangerous spaces that ordinary society eventually treats as resources. Both offer protagonists with extraordinary advantages.

That context matters.

When one story becomes globally enormous, audiences often begin treating its genre conventions as if they originated entirely with that property.

And now every Korean progression fantasy involving portals, systems or an overpowered male protagonist risks entering what we might call the Solo Leveling gravitational field.

Once something gets close enough, the comparison starts bending all conversation around it.

Tomb Raider King is currently sitting directly inside that gravity well.

The Audience Response Is Better Than the Discourse Makes It Sound

If you only encountered discussions calling Tomb Raider King a clone, you might assume audiences rejected it.

The actual numbers tell a different story.

Crunchyroll currently lists the show with an average audience score of 4.5 out of 5 from roughly 9,400 ratings.

IMDb’s early data has the series sitting around 7.1 out of 10.

Those numbers will naturally move as more episodes air and more viewers rate the series, but they establish something useful right now: people are watching it, and a substantial portion of the people watching it are enjoying it.

That does not invalidate criticism. But it does challenge the idea that Tomb Raider King has already been universally dismissed.

The audience reaction appears far closer to: “This looks familiar, but I’m having fun.”

The Animation Has a Different Battle to Fight

Perhaps the most unfair consequence of the Solo Leveling comparison involves production expectations.

The Solo Leveling anime became an event partly because A-1 Pictures understood exactly where to spend its visual ammunition.

Tomb Raider King comes from STUDIO EEK, with Seung Wook Woo serving as director and series composer. Hyun Joung Lee is credited as character designer, while Ju Young Kim provides the music.

The production has generally looked polished enough to sell its world, but this is not a project whose entire identity depends on transforming every confrontation into an animation showcase.

If you enter Tomb Raider King expecting every tomb encounter to recreate the visual intensity of Jinwoo’s largest battles, you may be disappointed.

But that may also be asking the adaptation to win the wrong contest.

Tomb Raider King needs its tombs to feel mysterious. It needs relics to have personality. It needs Jooheon’s schemes to remain understandable. And it needs enough action to keep the supernatural treasure hunt exciting.

The Source Material Has a Real Problem the Anime Must Solve

Tomb Raider King is long.

The manhwa extends for hundreds of chapters, and readers have repeatedly criticized portions of the story for becoming repetitive.

The basic cycle is vulnerable to repetition: a relic becomes relevant, someone wants it, Jooheon remembers something they do not, the situation flips, and Jooheon gains another advantage.

For readers who love highly competent protagonists dominating increasingly complicated scenarios, that loop is comfort food. For everyone else, it can eventually feel predictable.

That makes adaptation pacing extremely important.

Animation has a weapon the original serialized format did not: compression.

If STUDIO EEK identifies which confrontations matter, combines repetitive material and keeps the relic progression moving, the animated version could potentially become a more streamlined version of the story.

Then There Is the Anime Versus Aeni Question

Tomb Raider King has also wandered into another cultural conversation.

Crunchyroll has described the project as a South Korean animated adaptation, and STUDIO EEK is a South Korean animation studio.

That has led some communities to categorize the series as aeni, a term associated with Korean animation, rather than Japanese anime.

The argument points toward something larger.

The global animation landscape is changing. Korean webtoons are being adapted into television series. Chinese animation is increasingly finding international streaming audiences. Studios outside Japan are producing work that visually overlaps heavily with what international viewers colloquially call anime.

Tomb Raider King therefore represents something potentially more significant than simply finding viewers between seasons of Solo Leveling.

It is another step toward a media ecosystem where anime-influenced action series may originate throughout Asia rather than primarily Japan.

That Is Why “Solo Leveling Replacement” Feels Too Small

“Watch this if you like Solo Leveling” is useful.

“This exists primarily as a replacement for Solo Leveling” is not.

One opens a doorway. The other builds a cage.

Tomb Raider King becomes more interesting when you stop asking whether Jooheon Suh can become another Sung Jinwoo.

He does not need to.

Jinwoo’s story asks what happens when the weakest hunter receives the ability to grow without conventional limits.

Jooheon’s story asks what happens when somebody who lost the first game gets to restart before everyone else knows the rules.

One hero gains levels.

The other gains leverage.

RogueVerse Take: Give the King His Own Tomb

Tomb Raider King does not need protection from criticism.

Its similarities to other progression fantasies are obvious. Its protagonist will not work for everyone. The source material’s reputation for repetition is worth watching. The animation may never reach the spectacle associated with the biggest episodes of Solo Leveling.

Those are legitimate concerns.

But dismissing the series solely as a substitute for something more popular misses what makes it appealing.

The relic economy creates its own identity. Regression changes the shape of the power fantasy. Jooheon’s morally flexible approach makes him fundamentally different from a conventional heroic lead.

And the South Korean production itself represents an intriguing development in the expanding international ecosystem surrounding anime, aeni, manhwa and streaming animation.

Maybe the best way to approach Tomb Raider King is therefore not: “Is this the next Solo Leveling?”

Try this instead:

“Would it be fun to watch an experienced supernatural treasure hunter replay history while everyone else is still learning that treasure hunting exists?”

If the answer is yes, there is your recommendation.

Because sometimes the best thing a so-called replacement can do is refuse the job.

Tomb Raider King does not need to replace Solo Leveling.

It just needs enough viewers to realize there is more than one throne in the room.
