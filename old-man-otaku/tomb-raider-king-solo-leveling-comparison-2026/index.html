Tomb Raider King Is More Than a Solo Leveling Replacement, and That Comparison May Be Hurting It

Crunchyroll’s newest Korean action-fantasy has all the ingredients needed to trigger Solo Leveling comparisons. But beneath the supernatural artifacts and overwhelmingly capable protagonist is a very different power fantasy built around regression, revenge, information and getting to the treasure first.

By RogueVerse Studio | August 5, 2026

Spoiler level: Light spoilers for the opening premise and early episodes.

There may be no faster way to get an action-fantasy series noticed in 2026 than calling it “the next Solo Leveling.”

There may also be no faster way to make audiences judge it for everything it is not.

That is the uncomfortable position currently occupied by Tomb Raider King, the new South Korean animated adaptation streaming on Crunchyroll.

The comparisons were practically inevitable.

Its protagonist is a dark-haired Korean man operating in a modern world transformed by supernatural phenomena. Mysterious locations appear across the planet. Those locations contain objects capable of granting extraordinary powers. People enter them in pursuit of wealth and influence. A disastrous expedition leaves the protagonist facing death, only for something impossible to happen that gives him an enormous advantage over everyone around him.

Put those ingredients next to Solo Leveling and the family resemblance is difficult to ignore.

Some viewers have gone considerably further than noticing similarities.

Early online discussion has included accusations that Tomb Raider King is essentially a Solo Leveling clone with tombs replacing dungeons and relics replacing artifacts. One discussion following the premiere specifically compared the two stories point by point, from mysterious structures appearing around the world to supernatural interfaces and a protagonist receiving another chance after a lethal encounter.

That reaction is understandable.

It is also incomplete.

Because the deeper Tomb Raider King moves into its actual premise, the less useful the Solo Leveling comparison becomes.

The two stories may shop at the same supernatural hardware store, but they are building different houses.

What Is Tomb Raider King?

Tomb Raider King began as a South Korean web novel by SAN.G before receiving a manhwa adaptation by Yuns of REDICE STUDIO with artwork by 3B2S. Yen Press publishes the English edition of the manhwa.

Its world changes when mysterious tombs begin appearing across the globe.

These are not ordinary archaeological discoveries.

Inside are ancient supernatural relics capable of granting their owners extraordinary abilities.

Humanity responds about as gracefully as humanity normally does whenever an object capable of rearranging the balance of power appears.

People fight over them.

Corporations attempt to monopolize them.

The wealthy become even more powerful.

Governments and organizations become interested.

Raiders emerge to locate, enter and exploit tombs.

A completely new economy forms around supernatural artifacts.

At the center of that system is Jooheon Suh.

Jooheon is an experienced raider who has spent years working inside the dangerous world created by the tombs. Unlike an inexperienced newcomer wandering into an impossible situation, he understands what relics can do and knows how lethal the people competing for them can become.

Then his employer betrays him.

Jooheon is left to die.

Instead, he receives something considerably more useful than survival.

He returns approximately fifteen years into the past.

The tombs have not yet transformed civilization.

The major players have not accumulated their supernatural arsenals.

People who will eventually control priceless relics do not even know those objects exist yet.

But Jooheon remembers.

Suddenly the man who previously existed underneath the system is standing at the starting line before the system has even been built.

That is the real hook of Tomb Raider King.

This is not simply a story about becoming powerful.

It is a story about knowing where power will appear before everybody else.

Crunchyroll describes Jooheon as using both fury and future knowledge to raid tombs before his rivals can reach them. That distinction fundamentally changes the fantasy at work.

Solo Leveling Is About Ascension. Tomb Raider King Is About Preemption.

The easiest way to explain the difference between Solo Leveling and Tomb Raider King is to look at the relationship each protagonist has with knowledge.

Sung Jinwoo begins near the bottom.

His reputation is terrible.

His physical ability is limited.

Danger surrounds him.

When the System changes his life, neither Jinwoo nor the audience fully understands where it will ultimately take him.

That uncertainty powers much of early Solo Leveling.

Every improvement matters because we remember where Jinwoo started.

Every new ability expands the ceiling.

Every battle raises the question of how much further he can climb.

Jooheon is different.

His second journey begins with information.

He already lived through the future.

He remembers people.

He remembers betrayals.

He remembers relics.

He understands the emerging power structure before the people who will eventually dominate it even realize that structure exists.

That turns Tomb Raider King into something closer to supernatural New Game Plus.

Imagine replaying a massive role-playing game while retaining your memory of every secret weapon, hidden boss, rare item and future betrayal.

The townspeople are still explaining how shops work.

You already know where the legendary equipment is buried.

That is Jooheon’s advantage.

His progression therefore does not deliver exactly the same fantasy as watching Jinwoo become stronger.

Jooheon is trying to arrive first.

And sometimes, arriving first is more powerful than having the biggest sword.

Knowledge Becomes a Superpower

One of the most interesting ideas inside Tomb Raider King is that information has tangible value.

Suppose two people are equally capable of retrieving a powerful relic.

One knows the relic will exist five years before the other person does.

They are not actually equal.

The person with information controls the board.

Jooheon’s future memories allow him to identify valuable opportunities before society understands their importance.

That produces something resembling supernatural insider trading.

A tomb that appears unimportant to everyone else may contain something Jooheon remembers becoming priceless.

A person who appears harmless today may become dangerous later.

A company that seems insignificant may eventually control enormous influence.

An enemy who believes they are meeting Jooheon for the first time may already be dealing with someone who remembers exactly what they did to him in another timeline.

That is deliciously unfair.

And unfairness is part of the appeal.

Power fantasies do not always work because audiences want perfectly balanced conflicts.

Sometimes they work because audiences enjoy watching somebody walk into a trap carrying the instruction manual.

Jooheon Is Also a Different Kind of Protagonist

This may be the biggest reason some viewers bounce off Tomb Raider King.

Jooheon is not especially interested in behaving like a traditional shining hero.

He can be ruthless.

He can be opportunistic.

He carries grudges.

His knowledge of the future does not automatically turn him into a benevolent guardian of humanity.

Sometimes he helps people.

Sometimes he manipulates situations.

Sometimes his motivations are deeply personal.

And sometimes his primary concern is making sure somebody else does not get what he wants first.

That personality produces a very different relationship between protagonist and viewer.

Sung Jinwoo’s coolness increasingly emerges from capability.

Jooheon’s appeal often emerges from audacity.

You are not necessarily watching because you want him to save everyone.

You may be watching because you want to see what outrageous advantage he exploits next.

For some viewers, that makes him entertaining.

For others, it makes him difficult to connect with.

Both responses are already visible in discussions surrounding the source material.

Longtime manhwa readers remain divided. Some love Jooheon precisely because he behaves like someone who has already survived this world once and has no intention of playing fairly the second time. Others argue that his personality and repeated dominance reduce emotional investment in the supporting cast.

That divide will probably follow the animated adaptation throughout its run.

But Yes, the Solo Leveling Similarities Are Real

Defending Tomb Raider King as its own story does not require pretending the comparisons are imaginary.

They are not.

Both properties come from the broader Korean web-fiction and manhwa ecosystem.

Both operate in modern settings disrupted by supernatural systems.

Both involve dangerous spaces that ordinary society eventually treats as resources.

Both offer protagonists with extraordinary advantages.

Both lean into the pleasure of watching one individual become increasingly difficult for existing power structures to contain.

Both even emerged from a period when Korean progression fantasy was rapidly developing recognizable conventions.

That context matters.

When one story becomes globally enormous, audiences often begin treating its genre conventions as if they originated entirely with that property.

The result is that every later adaptation gets measured against the biggest hit.

We have seen this happen repeatedly across entertainment.

Every magical-school story gets compared to another magical-school story.

Every superhero reconstruction gets attached to the most famous superhero reconstruction.

Every dark fantasy is assigned the nearest internationally successful cousin.

And now every Korean progression fantasy involving portals, systems or an overpowered male protagonist risks entering what we might call the Solo Leveling gravitational field.

Once something gets close enough, the comparison starts bending all conversation around it.

Tomb Raider King is currently sitting directly inside that gravity well.

The Audience Response Is Better Than the Discourse Makes It Sound

Here is where the narrative gets more interesting.

If you only encountered discussions calling Tomb Raider King a clone, you might assume audiences rejected it.

The actual numbers tell a different story.

Crunchyroll currently lists the show with an average audience score of 4.5 out of 5 from roughly 9,400 ratings.

IMDb’s early data has the series sitting around 7.1 out of 10, with Episode 2 scoring higher than the premiere among early voters.

Those numbers will naturally move as more episodes air and more viewers rate the series, but they establish something useful right now:

People are watching it.

And a substantial portion of the people watching it are enjoying it.

That does not invalidate criticism.

A 4.5 Crunchyroll audience rating does not magically erase complaints about derivative character designs, limited animation or repetitive storytelling.

But it does challenge the idea that Tomb Raider King has already been universally dismissed.

The audience reaction appears far closer to:

“This looks familiar, but I’m having fun.”

And sometimes that is enough to build momentum.

The Animation Has a Different Battle to Fight

Perhaps the most unfair consequence of the Solo Leveling comparison involves production expectations.

The Solo Leveling anime became an event partly because A-1 Pictures understood exactly where to spend its visual ammunition.

Major confrontations became spectacles.

Transformation moments landed with enormous audiovisual weight.

The anime did not merely translate the appeal of the manhwa.

It amplified it.

Tomb Raider King comes from STUDIO EEK, with Seung Wook Woo serving as director and series composer. Hyun Joung Lee is credited as character designer, while Ju Young Kim provides the music.

The production has generally looked polished enough to sell its world, but this is not a project whose entire identity depends on transforming every confrontation into an animation showcase.

A Rotten Tomatoes-listed review of the early season awarded it four out of five while specifically noting that the animation could appear limited in places, even while praising its character design and blend of CG with traditional animation.

That distinction matters.

If you enter Tomb Raider King expecting every tomb encounter to recreate the visual intensity of Jinwoo’s largest battles, you may be disappointed.

But that may also be asking the adaptation to win the wrong contest.

Tomb Raider King needs its tombs to feel mysterious.

It needs relics to have personality.

It needs Jooheon’s schemes to remain understandable.

And it needs enough action to keep the supernatural treasure hunt exciting.

Its strongest identity may ultimately depend less on “Can it animate bigger explosions?” and more on “Can it make acquiring the next relic irresistible?”

The Source Material Has a Real Problem the Anime Must Solve

There is one criticism that should not be buried beneath comparisons.

Tomb Raider King is long.

The manhwa extends for hundreds of chapters, and readers have repeatedly criticized portions of the story for becoming repetitive.

The basic cycle is naturally vulnerable to repetition:

A relic becomes relevant.

Someone wants it.

Jooheon remembers something they do not.

A confrontation occurs.

Jooheon flips the situation.

Jooheon gains another advantage.

Repeat.

For readers who love highly competent protagonists dominating increasingly complicated scenarios, that loop is practically comfort food.

For everyone else, it can eventually feel predictable.

That makes adaptation pacing extremely important.

Animation has a weapon the original serialized format did not.

Compression.

If STUDIO EEK identifies which confrontations matter, combines repetitive material and keeps the relic progression moving, the animated version could potentially become a more streamlined version of the story.

If it adapts repetitive stretches too literally, the same criticism that followed the manhwa will eventually reach the show.

The first season therefore has something to prove beyond whether it can attract Solo Leveling fans.

It has to show that the formula can evolve.

Then There Is the Anime Versus Aeni Question

Tomb Raider King has also wandered into another cultural conversation.

What exactly should viewers call it?

Crunchyroll has described the project as a South Korean animated adaptation, and STUDIO EEK is a South Korean animation studio.

That has led some communities to categorize the series as aeni, a term associated with Korean animation, rather than Japanese anime.

The distinction has had practical consequences.

Posts attempting to discuss Tomb Raider King on Reddit’s r/anime have been automatically removed because that community’s rules define anime around Japanese production criteria. Users have instead been directed toward r/aeni.

That moderation policy is specific to one community, but the argument points toward something much larger.

The global animation landscape is changing.

Anime has become an international storytelling language.

Korean webtoons are being adapted into television series.

Chinese animation is increasingly finding international streaming audiences.

Studios outside Japan are producing work that visually overlaps heavily with what international viewers colloquially call anime.

Streaming platforms place these titles beside Japanese productions.

Average viewers may not care which production passport a show carries.

Industry observers probably should.

Tomb Raider King therefore represents something potentially more significant than simply finding viewers between seasons of Solo Leveling.

It is another step toward a media ecosystem where the anime-influenced action series dominating streaming platforms may originate throughout Asia rather than primarily Japan.

Korea already supplies some of the stories.

Increasingly, it may supply more of the finished animation too.

That Is Why “Solo Leveling Replacement” Feels Too Small

The headline works.

Of course it works.

Someone finishes Solo Leveling and asks what to watch next.

Tomb Raider King appears.

Modern Korean fantasy?

Check.

Powerful protagonist?

Check.

Supernatural locations?

Check.

Monsters, artifacts and escalating abilities?

Absolutely.

Recommendation algorithms practically write the comparison themselves.

But recommendations and criticism serve different purposes.

“Watch this if you like Solo Leveling” is useful.

“This exists primarily as a replacement for Solo Leveling” is not.

One opens a doorway.

The other builds a cage.

Tomb Raider King becomes more interesting when you stop asking whether Jooheon Suh can become another Sung Jinwoo.

He does not need to.

His fantasy is different.

Jinwoo’s story asks what happens when the weakest hunter receives the ability to grow without conventional limits.

Jooheon’s story asks what happens when somebody who lost the first game gets to restart before everyone else knows the rules.

One hero gains levels.

The other gains leverage.

RogueVerse Take: Give the King His Own Tomb

Tomb Raider King does not need protection from criticism.

Its similarities to other progression fantasies are obvious.

Its protagonist will not work for everyone.

The source material’s reputation for repetition is worth watching.

The animation may never reach the spectacle associated with the biggest episodes of Solo Leveling.

Those are legitimate concerns.

But dismissing the series solely as a substitute for something more popular misses what makes it appealing.

The relic economy creates its own identity.

Regression changes the shape of the power fantasy.

Jooheon’s morally flexible approach makes him fundamentally different from a conventional heroic lead.

And the South Korean production itself represents an intriguing development in the expanding international ecosystem surrounding anime, aeni, manhwa and streaming animation.

Crunchyroll’s current 4.5-out-of-5 audience rating suggests enough viewers are already finding something they enjoy.

Maybe the best way to approach Tomb Raider King is therefore not:

“Is this the next Solo Leveling?”

Try this instead:

“Would it be fun to watch an experienced supernatural treasure hunter replay history while everyone else is still learning that treasure hunting exists?”

If the answer is yes, there is your recommendation.

Because sometimes the best thing a so-called replacement can do is refuse the job.

Tomb Raider King does not need to replace Solo Leveling.

It just needs enough viewers to realize there is more than one throne in the room.
