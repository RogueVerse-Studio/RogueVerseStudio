Beyond My Hero Academia: Is Horror Kōhei Horikoshi’s Next Era?

The creator of one of modern shōnen manga’s biggest superhero stories is returning to Weekly Shōnen Jump with a 61-page horror one-shot. Here is what has actually been announced—and why the genre change may fit him better than the headline suggests.

Kōhei Horikoshi is returning to Weekly Shōnen Jump, but he is not bringing another class of superheroes with him.

His newly announced manga, 笑わないでよ しじまさん—romanized as Warawanaide yo, Shijima-san and informally translated as Don’t Laugh, Shijima-san or Please Don’t Laugh, Shijima-san—is being described as a “boy-meets-girl horror” story.

That alone makes this one of the year’s most interesting manga announcements. Horikoshi spent a decade building My Hero Academia into a global franchise defined by colorful powers, heroic ideals and explosive battles. His first completely new published story after that era will apparently move into something quieter, stranger and much darker.

But before the hype train leaves the station wearing a villain cape, one important correction needs to be made.

This is a one-shot, not a confirmed new series

The official announcement is for a 61-page one-shot scheduled for the combined Issue 37/38 of Weekly Shōnen Jump, releasing in Japan on August 10, 2026. The story will receive center-color treatment. The Japanese wording means a 61-page story with featured color pages—not necessarily 61 pages printed entirely in color.

That distinction matters because several English-language headlines make the announcement sound like Horikoshi is already launching his next long-running serialization. He is not—at least not according to anything Shueisha or Jump has announced.

No chapter two, collected volume, anime adaptation or connection to the My Hero Academia universe has been revealed. There is not even an official English title yet. What we have is one large, self-contained story and a teaser illustration showing a male protagonist beside a mysterious heroine.

That is still a major return. It is simply a different kind of return than the louder headlines suggest. ORICON NEWS and Comic Natalie describe the project as a one-shot, while the official My Hero Academia account confirms the August 10 date and “boy-meets-girl horror” description.

Why horror makes sense for Horikoshi

At first glance, moving from superhero shōnen to horror looks like a sharp turn. Look more closely at Horikoshi’s artwork, though, and the road has been there the entire time.

My Hero Academia was bright on the surface, but some of its strongest images were built from horror. Horikoshi repeatedly used distorted anatomy, damaged bodies, invasive transformations, heavy shadow and expressions that pushed characters past normal human limits. His villains did not just look dangerous; many looked physically wrong in ways designed to make the reader uncomfortable.

That visual language appeared through decaying skin, multiplying hands, broken smiles, unnatural growth and bodies changing under the pressure of powers they could barely contain. Even when the story remained hopeful, Horikoshi understood how to make a page feel unsafe.

Horror could give him room to stop pulling that instinct back.

In a superhero manga, disturbing imagery usually serves the action, villain or escalation of a battle. In a horror story, the discomfort can become the point. A shadow does not need to hide an opponent waiting for the next fight. It can hide something the reader may never fully understand. A strange smile does not have to introduce a superpower. It can be frightening simply because somebody should not be smiling at all.

The “boy-meets-girl” framework makes that even more intriguing. The phrase usually suggests connection, romance or coming-of-age discovery. Adding horror creates an immediate question: is the girl somebody the protagonist needs to save, somebody he should fear—or both?

The title may be doing double duty

The most direct translation of Warawanaide yo, Shijima-san is something close to “Don’t Laugh, Shijima-san” or “Please Don’t Laugh, Shijima-san.” Shijima appears to be the heroine’s name, but the word shijima can also evoke silence or deep stillness in Japanese.

Because the title writes Shijima phonetically instead of using kanji that would settle the meaning, readers may be meant to hear both the name and the feeling of silence beneath it. That is a theory, not a secret synopsis. Still, “do not laugh,” a mysterious girl and an atmosphere of silence form a potent horror combination before the first page has arrived.

Is this the project Horikoshi mentioned in 2024?

After My Hero Academia ended in August 2024, Horikoshi said he intended to create a new manga title as soon as he could. He had considered dividing the work and focusing only on the art, but his editor encouraged him to continue handling the full creative process. Horikoshi said he was slowly working from the storyboard stage while completing his remaining My Hero Academia commitments. ORICON reported those comments in December 2024.

Could Shijima-san be that project? Possibly. A 61-page one-shot is substantial, and the timing makes the connection tempting. But nobody has confirmed they are the same project.

The story could be a finished version of that early work. It could also be a later experiment, an independent story Horikoshi wanted to tell or a pilot that Jump may evaluate for future expansion.

Manga history gives fans a reason to keep the pilot possibility in mind. Horikoshi’s 2008 one-shot My Hero contained creative DNA that eventually developed into My Hero Academia. That does not make every one-shot a hidden series proposal. It does show how a short work can become a laboratory for a new tone, character dynamic or world.

What this return could tell us

The biggest question may not be whether Shijima-san becomes a series. It may be what the story reveals about Horikoshi after ten years of drawing the same universe on a punishing weekly schedule.

A one-shot is perfect for a creative reset. Sixty-one pages are enough to build atmosphere, introduce a relationship and land a real ending without establishing years of power systems, rivalries and future arcs. Horikoshi can make one complete statement, see how readers respond and decide what comes next without promising another marathon.

If the horror succeeds, it could prove that the qualities readers loved in My Hero Academia were never limited to superheroes. Horikoshi’s real strengths include expressive faces, emotional vulnerability, instantly readable silhouettes, cinematic page composition and the ability to make a character’s inner damage visible on the outside. Every one of those tools can work in horror—maybe even better.

What remains unknown

  • A full plot synopsis
  • The characters’ names beyond the likely use of Shijima-san
  • Whether the horror is supernatural, psychological, romantic or body horror
  • An ongoing serialization
  • An official English title or simultaneous VIZ/MANGA Plus release
  • Any connection to My Hero Academia
  • A collected edition or adaptation

The accurate headline is not “Horikoshi launches his next major series.” It is this: the creator of My Hero Academia is returning with a long horror one-shot that may show us what his art looks like without superheroes holding the darkness back.

That is already enough.

The cape is off. The hallway lights are flickering. On August 10, we find out what is standing at the other end.
