WEBTOON’s Next Anime Wave Could Bring a Whole New Crowd to Comics

Crunchyroll, HAYATE and LINE Digital Frontier are developing a slate of adaptations—and the opportunity extends beyond the screen.

By Old Man Otaku · RogueVerse Studio · October 22, 2026

An anime catches your attention. You watch a few episodes, find a favorite character, and suddenly waiting another week feels unreasonable. So you look up the original comic.

That’s the possibility worth watching behind WEBTOON’s next wave of anime adaptations: viewers becoming readers, then finding more stories along the way.

On October 8, HAYATE, Crunchyroll and WEBTOON Entertainment subsidiary LINE Digital Frontier announced a partnership at New York Comic Con to develop potential anime adaptations of around 15 selected titles. HAYATE will lead development and production, with Crunchyroll streaming the resulting shows exclusively in select territories, according to the official announcement.

The wording matters. This is a development framework for approximately 15 potential adaptations. It doesn’t mean 15 finished shows have release dates.

The announcement highlighted The Remarried Empress, Teenage Mercenary and The Boxer, with new adaptations announced for The Remarried Empress and The Boxer and promotional updates for Teenage Mercenary.

Those titles also give RogueVerse a useful question to explore: how many different audiences could this wave reach?

The Remarried Empress opens a different door

The Remarried Empress brings a particularly interesting opportunity. Its fantasy-romance identity gives the lineup an entry point for viewers drawn to relationships, royal drama and emotional conflict. HAYATE lists both its adaptation and Teenage Mercenary for 2027; The Boxer is listed as coming soon.

Our read is that The Remarried Empress has room to build conversation around character choices as much as visual spectacle. A tense exchange can give fans plenty to discuss. Sometimes a room full of people being politely terrible to each other is all the action you need.

That’s potential, though. The adaptation still has to make those relationships work on screen. Attractive character designs can earn a first look; convincing performances and careful pacing give viewers a reason to stay.

One adaptation could help readers discover the others

Then there’s the wider opportunity from the original RogueVerse posts: one adaptation could help readers discover other comics.

Imagine someone watching The Remarried Empress, searching for its source material, and browsing other fantasy romances afterward. Another viewer could arrive through Teenage Mercenary or The Boxer and follow a different reading path. Each show could become an introduction to a catalog the viewer hadn’t explored.

Watch Teenage Mercenary’s official promo trailer ↗

That’s our analysis of the opportunity, rather than a measured result of this partnership. The announcement doesn’t establish how many viewers will become readers or whether attention will spread between titles.

Making that connection takes work. Viewers need to know where the original story is available. Creator credits should be easy to find. Official reading links, useful recommendations and accessible editions could help turn curiosity into a reading habit.

For independent creators, that’s the part worth paying attention to. A wider audience for digital comics could create more opportunities for discovery. But visibility for a handful of major adaptations doesn’t automatically reach everyone publishing on the same platform.

The useful questions come afterward. Are new readers exploring beyond the adapted titles? Are creators receiving clear recognition? Are people returning to read once the promotional push settles down?

The story still has to work on screen

Watch The Boxer’s official promo trailer ↗

The adaptations themselves also have to respect how their stories work. Vertical comics control pace through scrolling, spacing and reveals. Animation adds voices, movement, sound and a fixed running time. Moving between those formats requires choices about where to linger and what to compress.

A dramatic moment needs enough room to land. Otherwise, viewers may understand what happened without feeling why it matters.

That leaves this new partnership with an exciting opportunity and a practical test. It can introduce established digital stories to new audiences. Its longer-term value will depend on the quality of the adaptations and what fans discover after watching them.
