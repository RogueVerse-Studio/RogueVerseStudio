Atari Survived the Gaming Graveyard by Selling the Past Back to the Future

The company behind gaming’s earliest boom has returned to profitability through retro releases, acquisitions and a growing vault of neglected intellectual property.

Atari has spent decades being remembered more often than being taken seriously. Now, the ghost has found a pulse.

For the fiscal year ending March 2026, Atari reported €56 million, or about $65 million, in annual revenue. That represented 66 percent year-over-year growth and the company’s highest revenue in more than a decade. Atari also posted a profit of roughly €100,000 after recording a €12.6 million loss the previous year.

The profit is small, but the direction matters. Atari did not rebuild by trying to become the next blockbuster factory. It became a caretaker for forgotten games, specialist studios and dormant franchises.

More than a logo

Modern Atari owns Digital Eclipse, known for interactive historical collections, and Nightdive Studios, which specializes in restoring and remastering older PC and console games.

Those teams provide technical expertise that nostalgia alone cannot supply. Old games may have missing source code, expired tools, fragmented rights and compatibility problems. Restoring them requires equal parts engineering and digital archaeology.

Thunderful added scale

Atari raised its ownership in Swedish gaming group Thunderful to approximately 97 percent. Thunderful’s contribution helped increase Atari’s reported revenue and expanded the company beyond its traditional retro identity.

The move also adds debt, integration costs and management complexity. Acquisitions can make a company look larger before they make it healthier.

A mobile lane through Hipster Whale

Atari acquired Hipster Whale, the studio behind Crossy Road, for an initial $29.3 million. The acquisition gives Atari a stronger mobile-development foundation and a potential bridge between its historic catalog and younger players.

The opportunity is obvious: many classic arcade ideas translate naturally to short, readable mobile experiences. The danger is turning charming games into brightly colored vending machines.

Buying forgotten worlds

Atari also acquired rights to five games previously controlled by Ubisoft: Cold Fear, I Am Alive, Child of Eden, Grow Home and Grow Up.

This may be the clearest expression of Atari’s new identity. Large publishers accumulate catalogs filled with games too small to justify internal resources. Atari is positioning itself as a buyer for those stranded properties.

Preservation or recycling?

Commercial rereleases can preserve access, but preservation requires more than putting an old file in a storefront. It includes technical restoration, documentation, context and long-term compatibility.

Digital Eclipse and Nightdive give Atari credibility because their work often goes beyond a lazy nostalgia harvest. The company must continue investing in that depth.

Profitability is a checkpoint

A €100,000 profit on €56 million in revenue is a milestone, not a victory screen. Atari must prove that its studios can release consistently, acquired properties can generate sustainable returns and its growing obligations remain manageable.

RogueVerse verdict

Atari has accepted something many legacy brands resist: it does not need to become the dominant force it once was. It needs to become useful.

The company once helped invent gaming’s future. Now it is building a future by excavating the past.
