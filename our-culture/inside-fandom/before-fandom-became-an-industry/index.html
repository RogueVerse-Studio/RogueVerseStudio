Before Fandom Became an Industry

How fan culture traveled from clubs and zines to Comiket, social media and global geek culture.

Today fandom looks enormous. Movie studios unveil trailers at conventions. Anime announcements cross oceans instantly. Cosplayers become professional creators. Fan artists build businesses. People livestream reactions to announcements that are themselves being livestreamed.

The machine is loud. Its beginnings were much quieter.

Before feeds, there were clubs

Early science-fiction conventions grew from organized fan communities in the late 1930s. Worldcon and related gatherings gave readers places to meet writers, artists and one another around shared speculative interests. Fan-created publications, clubs and costuming grew alongside those communities.

Japan built a creator-to-fan ecosystem

In Japan, doujin culture developed its own powerful creator network. Comic Market became one of the most visible expressions of that tradition, built around creators distributing their work directly to other fans. The modern event still centers doujin circles even while incorporating corporations, international visitors and cosplay.

That structure matters. Fandom was never only about buying what large companies produced. It also created pathways for people to answer existing culture with art, fiction, commentary, parody and entirely new worlds.

The internet collapsed distance

Then fans stopped needing conventions or mail networks to find one another. Forums became social platforms. Fan sites became YouTube channels. Artwork moved to global image feeds. Anime clubs became Discord servers. Cosplay became short-form video.

Conventions did not disappear. They became the physical gatherings of communities that already existed digitally.

From online community back to physical space

China’s Bilibili ecosystem is a striking modern example. Online ACG communities now support huge offline events such as Bilibili World, spanning animation, gaming, virtual creators, markets, model hobbies and tabletop culture.

Old fandom often worked like this: meet physically, then stay connected remotely. Modern fandom frequently works in reverse: meet remotely, then eventually gather physically.

The ritual survives the platform

That is the thread connecting an early science-fiction club, a Comiket doujin circle, a Gen Con campaign group and somebody posting their first cosplay video today.

Fandom is not really a collection of products. It is a collection of people building social rituals around stories and ideas.

Movies change. Anime changes. Games change. Platforms definitely change. The ritual survives.

The RogueVerse take

That history matters because independent creators now have publishing tools earlier fan generations could barely imagine. A drawing can become a comic, a video, a community, merchandise and eventually an entire fictional world without first passing through a traditional media gatekeeper.

The distance between fan and creator has become remarkably small.

Sometimes it is one decision: “I think I want to make something too.”

That may be fandom’s most important legacy.
